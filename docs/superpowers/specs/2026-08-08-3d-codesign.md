# One layout problem, not three

## The boundary that costs us

The compiler currently draws a hard line between the inside of a gate and the
space between gates. `place_nor_gate` emits a fixed cell — inputs on West, East
and South, output on North, 3x3 / 5x3 / 5x5 by fan-in — and the router's job is
to deliver each signal to the face the cell insists on.

Both sides of that line are made of the same material: dust, repeaters, stone,
torches. The line is not physical. It is an artefact of treating a gate as a
reusable component.

What it costs, measured on the decoder:

| part | repeaters | share |
|---|---|---|
| gate-entry | 354 | 47% |
| track | 253 | 33% |
| bypass | 96 | 13% |
| column | 55 | 7% |
| ramp | 0 | 0% |

**Gate-entry is the largest single cost.** It is what it costs to walk a signal
around to a face the cell demands, then walk it again from that face to the
torch — two separately planned runs where one continuous run would do. Neither
the placer nor the router can see this, because it is a consequence of a
decision neither of them makes.

## Dissolving it

Expand all the way down to blocks. Then the only element with any *function* is
the redstone torch: it attaches to a block, and it goes dark when that block is
powered. Everything else is wire.

So:

> **A NOR gate is a torch plus its support block, where that support block is
> the single sink of a net with N sources.**

The gate disappears into the netlist as a kind of net. All dust — what used to
be "inside" a cell and what used to be "between" cells — is planned by one
router, with no interior and no exterior.

This is standard-cell versus full-custom layout. Cells exist because reusing a
verified layout is cheaper than solving the general problem every time; real
chips use them for almost everything and hand-build only the parts that matter
most. What makes full-custom worth considering here is that the general problem
is small — one functional primitive, four block kinds — and that the machinery
it needs already exists for other reasons.

## What already carries over

Two invariants were built to fix bugs and happen to be exactly what removing
cells requires:

- **Spacing.** Routes declare conductor cells and keep-out cells, with keep-out
  derived from `connectivity::dust_reach` rather than hand-written. It knows
  nothing about cells.
- **Connectivity.** `compile()` checks that the finished world's actual
  connectivity equals the netlist's intended connectivity, failing with
  `CompileError::ConnectivityViolation` naming cells and nets. It does not care
  whether the blocks came from a template.

The second is already a full-custom correctness check. It is why the previous
Y-stacking attempt's failure mode — two nets' dust silently merging, which took
hours of gate-by-gate comparison to find — would now report its own
coordinates.

## What this costs, honestly

`verify_connectivity` checks **connectivity**, not **function**. It can prove a
net reaches exactly the cells it should. It cannot prove a torch is inverting
anything.

Today that guarantee comes from the template: the cell is correct, so every
instance is correct. Remove the template and something has to take its place —
an invariant that each torch's support block is genuinely the merge point of
its inputs, and that no input touches the torch itself rather than its support.

That invariant does not exist yet. **Writing it is the price of this approach,
and it should be written before the first geometry depends on it**, not after.

## The rest of the layout problem

With cells gone, two decisions remain, and they are coupled.

### Layers, not two planes

Alternating Y bands with alternating preferred direction, joined by dust
staircases — the multi-layer metal stack of any physical design flow. The
analogy is unusually exact here, and favourable: a staircase costs **signal
strength but zero ticks**, so the via is cheaper than its silicon counterpart.

Today's gate plane at `GATE_Y = 1` and track plane at `TRACK_Y = 3` are the
two-layer case.

### Placement as an optimisation

An objective — wire length, or critical-path wire length — minimised over
positions in a volume, subject to signal flow and non-overlap.

Today placement is a sort: ASAP levelisation by logic depth, then barycentre
ordering within a row. `compute_asap_levels`'s doc comment records that ALAP and
slack-based variants were measured and lost — but all three assign levels by a
rule. None of them tries to put a torch near the torches it talks to. That has
never been attempted, and §7.1 of the original spec left the method undecided.

Levelisation survives as a **constraint** — a signal cannot be consumed before
it is produced — not as the answer.

## Why the ambition is justified by the numbers

A full day of routing-only work moved the decoder from 16,894 blocks / 124 ticks
to 16,654 / 122. Replacing the hand-written netlist with a Yosys/ABC one moved
it to 8,922 / 85.

Squeezing one layer while the layers around it hold the wrong assumptions has a
low ceiling. Gate-entry being the largest remaining cost is that ceiling made
visible: it is not a routing inefficiency, it is a boundary that should not
exist.

## How it gets judged

Every claim here is measurable before it is believed.

- `settle` ticks, predicted by `src/timing/`'s model and confirmed by the
  simulator. The model is asserted **exact** on all five circuits today and must
  stay exact — it is the instrument, and an instrument that is only usually
  right is worth little.
- `blocks/gate`, which tracks delay across circuits better than raw size.
- The per-part repeater breakdown from `routing_cost_report`. Note that
  "gate-entry" stops being a meaningful category once cells are gone; whatever
  replaces it has to be defined so the before/after stays comparable.
- The truth tables, and the RCON conformance run against a real 1.20.1 server.

Baseline, at `02110dd`:

| circuit | gates | blocks | settle | blocks/gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| and4 | 7 | 551 | 28 | 78.7 |
| full_adder | 22 | 2066 | 76 | 93.9 |
| segment_a | 46 | 6686 | 92 | 145.3 |
| seven_segment | 84 | 16654 | 122 | 198.3 |
| seven_segment (Verilog) | 40 | 8922 | 85 | 223.1 |

Horizontal extent must stay under the ticking-area bound the tests already
assert. Vertical extent gains a bound of its own: the build must fit in
Minecraft's world height with room to place it sensibly, and any report must say
what that height would be for a circuit ten times deeper.

## Order

Not one change, and not one commit. Each piece lands with its own measurement
against the baseline, and a piece that does not pay for itself is reverted
rather than carried — as ALAP scheduling was.

1. **The torch-merge invariant.** It replaces the guarantee the cell template
   was providing, and nothing else can safely land before it.
2. **Cells dissolved into the netlist.** Gates become nets with N sources and
   one sink; all dust is routed by one router. Expected to move gate-entry cost
   specifically.
3. **Layers.** Generalise two planes to many, with staircases as vias.
4. **Placement as optimisation.** The largest and the least certain; it needs
   the other three to have something worth optimising.

## Out of scope

- Logic optimisation. Yosys and ABC do it, and `f339c76` connected them.
- VHDL. The frontend seam is Yosys's JSON, so GHDL slots in downstream-free.
- The viewer, beyond keeping it able to draw what the compiler emits.
